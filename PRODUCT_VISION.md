# Cash Control Product Vision

## North star

Cash Control will be the best budgeting and cash-management app for college
students, especially students whose income changes from week to week or month
to month.

It should make money feel understandable and manageable. The interface stays
calm, clean, and focused while accounting and business-economics logic do the
serious work underneath it.

Cash Control is not a cheap classroom demo. It is a carefully crafted product
built to solve a real problem for real students while applying and extending
what its creator learns in college.

## Who we serve

Our primary user is a college student who may have:

- irregular income from hourly work, tips, gigs, internships, financial aid,
  scholarships, or family support;
- fixed obligations alongside unpredictable day-to-day expenses;
- limited time, limited financial experience, and little room for mistakes;
- a need to know what is safe to spend before the next income arrives;
- frustration with financial apps that feel crowded, technical, or demanding.

We design for this student first. We do not broaden the product at the expense
of their needs.

## Product promise

Cash Control helps a student answer four questions quickly and confidently:

1. How much usable cash do I have right now?
2. What must this cash cover before I am paid again?
3. What can I safely spend?
4. What changed, and what should I do next?

## The Cash Control method

Cash Control adapts the accounting cycle into a simple, repeating student
workflow:

1. **Start** — Establish the student's real opening position: available cash,
   debts, recurring obligations, and the next expected sources of income.
2. **Record** — Capture each financial event once in plain language, including
   income, purchases, borrowing, debt payments, and transfers.
3. **Categorize** — Classify activity consistently so the records can explain
   where money came from, where it went, and whether it changed cash, debt, or
   both.
4. **Review and reconcile** — Resolve pending, missing, duplicated, or
   incorrectly classified activity before relying on the results.
5. **Adjust** — Correct mistakes and account for student realities such as
   reimbursements, refunds, shared expenses, fees, and irregular income.
6. **Close the period** — Complete a lightweight weekly review and a more
   complete monthly review, preserving a trustworthy snapshot of what
   happened.
7. **Understand and act** — Turn the completed records into a few useful
   answers: usable cash, upcoming obligations, safe-to-spend money, spending
   patterns, debt movement, and the most important next action.
8. **Begin again** — Carry the ending position into the next period and use
   what was learned to make a better plan.

The transaction ledger is the factual foundation. Budgeting, forecasting,
alerts, goals, and guidance must build from the same reliable records.

The user should feel a natural rhythm—not like they are completing accounting
homework. Cash Control can use accounting terms internally while presenting
clear prompts such as "What happened?", "Does everything look right?", and
"Here is what you can safely use next."

## Reporting rhythm

Cash Control provides information at two different levels so students receive
useful answers without being overwhelmed.

### Weekly check-in

The weekly experience is short, practical, and focused on the next decision.
It should show:

- money received during the week;
- total spent and the largest spending categories;
- current usable cash;
- bills and other obligations due before the next likely income;
- a safe-to-spend amount for the near term;
- pending or uncategorized activity that needs attention;
- one or two clear observations or suggested actions.

The weekly check-in should take only a few minutes and end with a clear sense
of what the student can do next.

### Monthly review

The monthly experience is more complete and explains what changed during the
period. It should show:

- total income and funding, with borrowing kept separate from income;
- total expenses and a breakdown by meaningful student categories;
- food spending separated into groceries and dining out, with a combined food
  total available for a simpler view;
- housing, utilities, transportation, school, healthcare, subscriptions,
  entertainment, shopping, financial fees, and other spending;
- the change in cash, debt, and net financial position;
- debt principal paid and interest paid;
- recurring spending and notable changes from prior periods;
- actual results compared with the student's plan or available resources;
- a concise summary of what went well, what needs attention, and what to carry
  into the next month.

Comparisons should account for irregular income and different month lengths.
Cash Control should explain context instead of labeling a student as simply
"over budget" or "under budget."

### Information hierarchy

Reports should begin with a plain-language summary, then reveal category and
transaction detail on request. Charts and metrics are included only when they
make a pattern or decision easier to understand.

## Budgeting model

Budgeting is the forward-looking half of Cash Control. The ledger records what
happened; the budget gives available resources a purpose before they are used.
Both must share the same accounts, categories, dates, and source of truth.

### The master plan

Cash Control should maintain one connected **master plan** for the selected
period. “Master budget” may be shown as an optional learning term, but the
primary interface should use plain language.

The master plan combines:

- an income and funding plan;
- fixed bills and other committed expenses;
- flexible spending plans by category;
- minimum and additional debt payments;
- savings and cash-buffer contributions;
- goals for large or future purchases;
- a cash plan that tests whether money will be available when each item is due.

Changing one part should update the whole plan. A student should not have to
maintain disconnected budgets or enter the same amount more than once.

### Planning equation

The master plan should make this relationship visible:

```text
Cash available now
+ money expected during the period
- required bills and essential spending
- planned debt payments
- planned savings and goal contributions
- selected safety buffer
= flexible money left to plan
```

Expected amounts, confirmed amounts, and actual transactions must remain
visibly distinct. The plan should warn about timing problems even when the
period's total income appears sufficient.

### Budgets within the master plan

Students may create focused plans within the master plan for:

- groceries, dining out, transportation, entertainment, and other spending
  categories;
- emergency savings or a general cash buffer;
- tuition, books, moving, travel, a vehicle, a laptop, or another large
  purchase;
- debt payoff beyond required minimum payments.

These plans are not separate sources of money. They allocate resources already
included in the master plan, preventing the same cash from being promised to
multiple purposes.

### Savings and purchase goals

A goal should include a name, target amount, target date, current amount, and
priority. Cash Control can calculate a suggested weekly or monthly
contribution and show the tradeoff created by changing the amount or date.

Moving cash into a savings account is a transfer, not an expense. Cash Control
should track the amount assigned to a goal separately from the bank account
that holds it. When a planned purchase occurs, the purchase becomes the
expense; moving the saved cash must not cause it to be counted twice.

### Budget versus actual

As transactions are recorded and categorized, Cash Control should update each
plan automatically. For each relevant category or goal, show:

- planned amount;
- actual amount;
- amount remaining or contributed;
- dollar difference from the plan;
- pace or projected result when it is genuinely useful.

Use contextual language such as “$24 remaining,” “$18 more than planned,” or
“on pace to reach this goal.” Avoid unexplained favorable/unfavorable labels
and judgmental red-versus-green scoring.

### Rolling decisions

A budget should remain useful when reality changes. Students should be able to
revise future allocations while preserving the original plan and actual
results for honest comparison. Cash Control should explain the tradeoff when
money is moved—for example, adding to dining out may reduce the amount
available for a laptop goal or cash buffer.

At the end of a period, unspent amounts should not roll forward silently. Let
the student choose whether to carry them forward, move them to a goal, add them
to a buffer, pay debt, or return them to unallocated cash.

## Product principles

### Student-simple, financially sound

Use familiar language and reveal complexity only when it helps. The student
should not need accounting knowledge, but the calculations must withstand
accounting scrutiny.

### Decisions before dashboards

Show the next useful decision before secondary statistics. A screen should not
display information merely because it can be calculated.

### Irregular income is the default

Do not assume equal paychecks, a stable monthly salary, or a four-week month.
Planning should work from known cash, expected income ranges, due dates, and
the time until the next likely income event.

### Calm progressive disclosure

Each screen should have one clear purpose and one obvious next action. Start
with the essential answer, then let users open details when they need them.

### One source of truth

Users should record an event once. Balances, budgets, forecasts, and reports
should update from that shared record rather than requiring duplicate entry.

### Explain the reasoning

When Cash Control recommends an action or labels money as safe to spend, it
should make the reasoning understandable. Avoid unexplained scores and black
boxes.

### Respect limited cash and attention

Avoid shame, alarmist language, manipulative engagement, and unnecessary
notifications. Help students recover from mistakes and make the next good
decision.

### Craft over feature count

A smaller set of coherent, reliable tools is better than many shallow tools.
Every feature should feel intentional, tested, and connected to the method.

## Two-layer experience

Cash Control has two inseparable layers:

1. **The accounting backbone** maintains accurate classifications, balances,
   period activity, and relationships between cash, income, expenses, and
   debt.
2. **The student interface** translates those mechanics into clean screens,
   familiar language, guided actions, and useful explanations.

Simplifying the interface must never mean weakening the accounting. Preserving
the accounting must never be used as a reason to make the interface difficult.

### Language standard

Lead with the clearest everyday explanation. Introduce the accounting term
only when it helps the student learn, and provide more detail on request.

Examples include:

- **Money in and money out**, with “cash flow” shown as the accounting term;
- **Money you owe**, with “liabilities” available as a learning label;
- **Check that everything matches**, instead of leading with “reconcile”;
- **Difference from your plan**, before introducing “variance”;
- **Finish your weekly or monthly review**, instead of leading with “close the
  period”;
- **Amount that reduces what you owe** and **cost of borrowing**, before
  expecting students to understand principal and interest.

Explanations should be short, contextual, and connected to the number or
action in front of the student. Definitions should not interrupt the primary
task. A “Why?” or “Learn what this means” control can reveal the accounting
reasoning when wanted.

### Interface standard

- Give each screen one primary purpose and one obvious next action.
- Use whitespace, hierarchy, and progressive disclosure instead of dense
  dashboards.
- Show a plain-language conclusion before supporting calculations.
- Ask one manageable question at a time during setup and transaction entry.
- Use consistent labels, categories, colors, and interaction patterns.
- Reserve warnings for conditions that genuinely require attention.
- Make corrections easy and nonjudgmental.
- Keep advanced details available without making them the default view.

## Accounting and economics foundation

The product should consistently distinguish:

- income from borrowing;
- expenses from debt-principal payments;
- cash movement from economic gain or loss;
- posted activity from pending activity;
- current cash from cash that is already committed;
- fixed obligations from flexible choices;
- historical results from forecasts and assumptions.

Business-economics reasoning should help users understand scarcity,
opportunity cost, tradeoffs, uncertainty, timing, and incentives without
turning the app into a textbook.

## What Cash Control is not

Cash Control is not:

- a generic finance app designed for every possible customer;
- a dense accounting system that expects students to learn jargon;
- a dashboard crowded with every available metric;
- a rigid monthly budget that treats irregular income as an exception;
- an investment, tax, or lending-advice product;
- a collection of unrelated calculators or classroom exercises.

## Feature decision filter

Before adding or changing anything, ask:

1. Does this solve an important problem for a college student?
2. Does it work well for irregular income?
3. Does it make a decision clearer or easier?
4. Is the underlying accounting and economic reasoning correct?
5. Can the experience be simpler without losing necessary truth?
6. Does it fit the Cash Control method and the existing source of truth?
7. Can we implement and test it with the level of craft a real product needs?

If a proposal does not pass these questions, it should be revised, deferred,
or rejected.

## Current product direction

The existing accounting ledger is the foundation, not the finished
experience. The next stage should translate that foundation into a simpler
student workflow centered on:

- getting started without accounting setup anxiety;
- seeing usable cash and near-term obligations immediately;
- planning safely between irregular income events;
- recording activity quickly;
- receiving a small number of clear, explainable insights;
- completing a calm weekly review.

This document is the standing product direction for Cash Control. Changes to
the audience, promise, or principles should be deliberate and documented here
before they redirect the product.

## First-run experience

The first experience should introduce Cash Control briefly, learn what the
student needs, establish a reliable starting position, and then provide an
immediately useful financial picture. It should not open on an empty dashboard
or begin by asking the student to understand accounting terminology.

### 1. Introduce Cash Control

The welcome screen should explain the product in a few sentences:

> Cash Control helps you understand your money and make more confident
> decisions. See what comes in, where it goes, and what needs your attention
> through simple weekly check-ins and monthly reviews.

The primary action should be **Get started**. A secondary action may let the
student explore with sample data.

The screen should set expectations about how long setup takes, what the
student will receive from it, and where their data is stored.

### 2. Understand the student's needs

Before collecting detailed amounts, ask what the student most wants help with.
Examples include:

- making money last until the next income;
- keeping up with bills and due dates;
- understanding where money goes;
- controlling flexible spending;
- reducing debt;
- building a cash buffer;
- handling financial aid or other irregular funding.

Use these answers to emphasize the most useful information later. Do not
pretend that a short questionnaire creates a complete financial profile.

### 3. Establish the starting position

Guide the student through one manageable question at a time:

1. **Where do you keep money?** Identify checking, savings, and physical cash
   without leading with the term "asset account."
2. **How much is available today?** Record opening cash balances and the date
   they were checked.
3. **How does money usually come in?** Learn whether income is steady,
   irregular, seasonal, dependent on tips or gigs, or received through aid,
   scholarships, grants, or family support.
4. **What money might come in next?** Capture expected dates, reasonable amount
   ranges, and how certain each estimate is.
5. **What must be paid?** Identify rent, utilities, phone, insurance, tuition,
   subscriptions, minimum debt payments, and other recurring obligations with
   amounts and due dates.
6. **What flexible essentials do you need?** Estimate groceries,
   transportation, healthcare, school supplies, and other near-term needs.
7. **What money do you owe?** Identify credit cards, loans, unpaid bills, and
   other debts in plain language.

Students should be able to skip information they do not know and return to it
later. Estimates must be labeled as estimates rather than mixed with posted
transactions or confirmed balances.

### 4. Confirm before calculating

Show a concise review of the student's starting cash, expected money in,
upcoming obligations, essential estimates, and debts. Let the student correct
anything easily before Cash Control uses it.

### 5. Deliver the first useful picture

After setup, explain:

- cash available now;
- confirmed obligations and essential needs before the next likely income;
- expected income and its level of certainty;
- the number of days the current money may need to last;
- an estimated safe-to-spend amount with a visible calculation;
- the first recommended action.

The result should reflect the needs the student selected during setup. Cash
Control should continue learning from confirmed transactions and reviews
rather than requiring perfect information on the first day.
